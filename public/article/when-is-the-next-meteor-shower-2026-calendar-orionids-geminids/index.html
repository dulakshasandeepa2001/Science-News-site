When Is the Next Meteor Shower? 6 Celestial Displays Lighting the Sky Before 2027

Wondering if there is a meteor shower tonight? While the August Perseids have concluded, the final four months of 2026 deliver an extraordinary sequence of night sky events: six major meteor showers, Halley's Orionids, Taurid fireballs, the 120/hr Geminids under moonless skies, a four-planet alignment, and the Beaver Supermoon.

1.Autumn Skywatching Overview: 6 Celestial Displays Before 2027

The arrival of astronomical autumn extends nighttime hours across the northern hemisphere, prompting millions of stargazers to search when is the next meteor shower and whether there is a meteor shower tonight.

While the August Perseids have concluded, the final four months of 2026 offer an extraordinary sequence of skywatching spectacles: six major meteor showers, a four-planet parade, and the November Beaver Supermoon.

From the fireball-rich Taurid twins to the prolific Geminids—which arrive under ideal moonless conditions this December—here is the complete observation timetable, peak dates, hourly counts, and astronomical guide for watching the night sky light up before the new year.

2.1. Quick Schedule: Major Meteor Showers of Late 2026

The observation timetable below outlines every major meteor shower active through the remainder of the year:

• Draconids (Active Oct 6–10 | Peak: Oct 8): ~5 meteors/hr from Comet 21P/Giacobini-Zinner. Best in early evening immediately after sunset; minimal moon interference.

• Orionids (Active Oct 2–Nov 12 | Peak: Oct 21–22): ~20 meteors/hr originating from Halley's Comet (1P/Halley). Fast streaks leaving persistent ionization trains; waxing gibbous moon sets before peak predawn viewing.

• Southern Taurids (Active Sept 20–Nov 20 | Peak: Nov 4–5): ~5 meteors/hr from Comet 2P/Encke. Broad multi-night plateau; exceptionally high fireball frequency casting visible shadows.

• Northern Taurids (Active Oct 20–Dec 10 | Peak: Nov 11–12): ~5 meteors/hr associated with Asteroid 2004 TG10. Overlaps with Southern Taurids to deliver brilliant fireball streaks.

• Leonids (Active Nov 6–30 | Peak: Nov 16–17): 10–15 meteors/hr from Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle. Hyper-fast shooting stars (70 km/s) colliding head-on with Earth's atmosphere.

• Geminids (Active Dec 4–20 | Peak: Dec 13–14): 120 meteors/hr from Asteroid 3200 Phaethon. The marquee astronomical event of 2026; a new moon creates pitch-dark skies for multicolored shooting stars.

• Ursids (Active Dec 17–26 | Peak: Dec 21–22): ~10 meteors/hr from Comet 8P/Tuttle. Coincides with the Winter Solstice, with best activity concentrated in predawn hours.

3.2. October: The Early Draconids and Halley's Orionids

October initiates the primary meteor season with two contrasting displays: one best viewed at sunset, and the other originating from the most famous comet in astronomical history.

The Draconids (Peak: October 8) Originating from the northern constellation Draco the Dragon, the Draconids represent the briefest major shower of the autumn slate, lasting just five nights from October 6 through 10. While typical showings produce a modest rate of around 5 meteors per hour, the Draconids have an unusual viewing schedule: while most meteor showers require waking up after midnight, the radiant point of Draco sits highest in the sky immediately after sunset. Skywatchers can catch streaks of light during the early evening hours without waiting for late-night hours.

The Orionids (Peak: October 21–22) The Orionids are the second annual meteor shower created by the debris field of Halley's Comet (1P/Halley). Each year, Earth intersects the trail of ice and rock particles deposited by the comet across centuries of solar orbits:

• Observation Timing: The shower remains active from October 2 through November 12, culminating during the overnight hours of October 21 into the early morning of October 22.

• Expected Rates: Observers under clear rural skies can see up to 20 meteors per hour.

• Viewing Dynamics: Orionid meteors travel at roughly 66 km per second, frequently leaving glowing persistent ionization trains that hang in the upper atmosphere. The radiant near Orion’s club rises in the east-southeast after 10:00 PM, with peak activity occurring in the hours before dawn. The timing also avoids the freezing temperatures common during December showers.

4.3. November: Fireball Twins, Fast Leonids, and the Beaver Supermoon

November provides an overlapping mix of meteors alongside high-profile lunar and planetary events.

The Southern and Northern Taurids (Peaks: Nov 4–5 and Nov 11–12) The Taurids stem from a complex stream of debris linked to the fragmentation of an ancient progenitor comet. The stream has split into two separate paths: the Southern Taurids (originating from Comet 2P/Encke) and the Northern Taurids (associated with near-Earth asteroid 2004 TG10).

While overall rates average a modest 5 meteors per hour, the Taurid complex is famous for generating fireballs—exceptionally large, bright meteors that outshine Venus and briefly cast visible shadows. Because both showers exhibit broad, multi-night plateaus rather than sharp single-night peaks, skywatchers have multiple opportunities to plan around localized cloud forecasts.

The Leonids (Peak: November 16–17) Radiating from the constellation Leo, the Leonids are historically famous for producing rare "meteor storms" (exceeding 1,000 meteors per hour in 1833, 1966, and 2001). While 2026 will bring a typical display of 10 to 15 meteors per hour, the Leonids remain among the fastest shooting stars in astronomy, colliding with Earth's atmosphere head-on at 70 km per second.

Planetary Parade & The Beaver Supermoon Beyond shooting stars, November features two additional sky milestones:

• The Four-Planet Gathering (Mid-November): Around November 14, early risers can view Mercury, Venus, Mars, and Jupiter aligned above the eastern horizon before sunrise. Mars and Jupiter reach a tight conjunction on November 14 before switching relative positions.

• The Beaver Supermoon (November 24): The November Full Moon occurs near perigee, appearing roughly 13% brighter than an average full moon and kicking off a sequence of four consecutive supermoons extending into early 2027.

5.4. December: The Marquee Geminids and the Solstice Ursids

December delivers the most active meteor shower of the entire year, concluding with a display timed alongside the Winter Solstice.

The Geminids: The King of 2026 Showers (Peak: December 13–14) Unlike the majority of meteor showers that originate from icy comets, the Geminids trace their ancestry to 3200 Phaethon, a dense, rocky asteroid that sheds debris along an eccentric sun-grazing orbit:

• High Hourly Output: During its peak on the night of December 13 into December 14, observers in dark-sky locations can expect up to 120 meteors per hour—producing one to two visible shooting stars every single minute.

• Optimal Lunar Phase: In 2026, the peak occurs just days after a new moon. Without bright moonlight washing out the night sky, faint meteors and distinct color tints (yellow, green, and white) will remain clearly visible.

• Evening Visibility: Gemini rises early in the eastern sky, meaning meaningful meteor activity starts as early as 9:00 PM, offering great viewing for families before late-night cold sets in.

The Ursids (Peak: December 21–22) Arriving on the exact night of the Winter Solstice, the Ursids provide the final celestial curtain call of 2026. Radiating from Ursa Minor near Polaris, this circumpolar shower yields roughly 10 meteors per hour, with the best viewing concentrated in the hours before daybreak.

6.5. Pro Stargazing Guide: How to Maximize Visible Meteors

To see the highest volume of meteors during any active peak, use these observing practices:

• Escape Urban Light Domes: Streetlights and suburban lighting drown out the vast majority of faint meteors. Travel to rural parks, elevated terrain, or designated Dark Sky sites.

• Do Not Use a Telescope or Binoculars: Meteor showers streak across wide sections of the sky. Optical magnification narrows your field of view down to a tiny point, making it almost impossible to catch moving meteors. The naked eye is the most effective tool.

• Allow 20 to 30 Minutes of Dark Adaptation: The human eye takes up to half an hour to adjust to low-light conditions. Looking at a bright smartphone screen resets this adaptation immediately; use red-light filters if illumination is necessary.

• Monitor Local Cloud Forecasts: Check satellite radar and cloud-cover maps on weather apps a few hours prior to heading out to locate clear pockets in the atmosphere.

• Prepare for Thermal Comfort: Autumn and winter nighttime temperatures drop quickly. Dress in heavy thermal layers, bring an insulated ground pad or reclining lawn chair, and use a sleeping bag to observe the sky comfortably without straining your neck.
