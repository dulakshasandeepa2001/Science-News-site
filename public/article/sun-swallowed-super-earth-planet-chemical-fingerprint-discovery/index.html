Did Our Sun Swallow a Super-Earth? Inside the 4-Billion-Year-Old Cosmic Murder Mystery

A groundbreaking study published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society reveals that our infant Sun likely engulfed a rocky super-Earth between 5 and 10 Earth masses, solving the decades-old solar lithium depletion paradox and convection zone sound-speed anomalies.

✍️ By: Astrophysics & Solar Physics Research Desk 📅 Published: September 12, 2026 ⏱️ Read Time: 7 min read

1.Did Our Sun Swallow a Super-Earth? Inside the Cosmic Murder Mystery

For decades, exoplanet surveys conducted by orbital observatories like NASA’s Kepler and TESS have revealed a perplexing pattern across the cosmos: super-Earths—rocky worlds measuring roughly 2 to 10 times the mass of our home planet—are among the most common planetary classes in the Milky Way.

Yet, when planetary scientists gaze across our own solar system, this ubiquitous cosmic archetype is conspicuously missing. Why did our sun spawn tiny terrestrial inner worlds like Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars, alongside outer gas giants, while completely skipping the universe's most popular planetary design?

According to an astrophysics study published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (MNRAS), the answer may be far darker than previously imagined: our infant Sun may have eaten it.

Led by astrophysicist Dr. Mutlu Yildiz of Ege University in Turkey, advanced evolutionary modeling indicates that roughly four billion years ago, a massive rocky world between 5 and 10 times the mass of Earth migrated inwards through the primordial protoplanetary disk and plunged directly into the young Sun. Crucially, the study suggests that the structural and chemical "fingerprints" of this devoured world may still be buried deep within the solar interior.

2.1. Fact Breakdown: The Missing Super-Earth & The Solar Interior

Key findings and astrophysics parameters documented in the Ege University study:

• Devoured World Mass: Estimated between 5 to 10 times Earth’s mass (a textbook super-Earth). • Time of Engulfment: Billions of years ago during the infant Sun's initial accretion and disk-clearing phase. • Lead Author & Institution: Dr. Mutlu Yildiz, Department of Astronomy and Space Sciences, Ege University, Turkey. • Publication Journal: Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (MNRAS). • Primary Anomalies Resolved: Solves the Sun's depleted surface lithium paradox and rectifies discrepancies in the sound-speed profile beneath the solar convection zone. • Modeling Tool: Advanced open-source stellar evolution and helioseismology simulation software.

3.2. Two Decades-Old Solar Puzzles Solved at Once

The planetary engulfment hypothesis was not devised simply to explain the absence of a local super-Earth; it emerged as an elegant, unified solution to two long-standing contradictions in solar physics:

• The Missing Lithium Dilemma: Lithium, forged during the Big Bang and fundamental to early cosmic nucleosynthesis, is present in expected quantities across young, sun-like stars throughout the galaxy. However, direct spectroscopic scans show that our Sun's outer surface contains a stark depletion of lithium compared to its stellar peers. • The Convection Zone Sound-Speed Discrepancy: Standard solar models have persistently struggled to reproduce helioseismology data—the study of acoustic sound waves propagating through the Sun. Independent measurements of sound speeds just beneath the solar convection zone deviate noticeably from theoretical stellar physics models.

Dr. Yildiz hypothesized that these seemingly unrelated structural anomalies shared a single, violent catalyst. Because forming planetary bodies condense out of refractory materials and heavy elements distinct from the volatile gases of the surrounding nebula, the direct ingestion of a rocky planet would fundamentally recalibrate a young star's interior chemical ratios and opacity profiles.

4.3. Simulating the Fall: How a Planet Plunges into Its Host Star

During the earliest epochs of the solar system, the infant Sun was encircled by an expansive, dense protoplanetary disk composed of gas, ice, and dust. Previous dynamical simulations have shown that planetary embryos frequently formed within or near the current orbit of Mercury.

Through gravitational drag against the gas-rich disk—a process known as orbital migration—growing planets often lose angular momentum, spiraling inward toward their parent star.

Using open-source stellar evolution software, Yildiz simulated hundreds of accretion trajectories and chemical permutations to evaluate how the Sun would respond to swallowing various celestial objects. When the algorithm simulated the ingestion of a super-Earth sized between 5 and 10 Earth masses, the simulated solar interior snapped into near-perfect alignment with modern helioseismology readings and observed surface lithium concentrations.

"We thought planetary engulfment might affect the solar structure but did not expect the calculations to converge on such a specific super-Earth mass range," Dr. Yildiz noted. "That was one of the most interesting outcomes of the study. The ingestion of a super-Earth could help explain long-standing differences between standard solar models and observations."

5.4. Cosmic Precedent: Stars Caught in the Act of Planetary Feasts

While direct evidence from our Sun requires subsurface acoustic verification, astronomers have already witnessed similar planetary cannibalism in deep space:

• The Aquila Star Engulfment (2023): In 2023, astronomers utilizing the Palomar Observatory and infrared space telescopes recorded a Sun-like star approximately 12,000 light-years away in the constellation Aquila swallowing a gas giant planet the size of Jupiter. That event produced an energetic optical outburst followed by a lingering infrared dust trail, proving that stars regularly consume their surrounding broods when orbits destabilize. • White Dwarf Atmospheric Pollution: Spectroscopic surveys of white dwarfs across the galaxy routinely reveal atmospheres "polluted" with iron, silicon, and magnesium from disrupted rocky bodies that fell into the star.

The difference in our solar system's history is the timing: our Sun’s planetary meal took place during its turbulent youth, altering its chemical baseline before the remaining planets stabilized into the calm, predictable orbits we navigate today.

6.5. How Scientists Plan to Verify the 'Stellar Crime'

The findings presented in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society do not claim definitive confirmation; rather, they present a robust, mathematically rigorous framework that elevates planetary engulfment from wild speculation to a mathematically plausible model.

The next step involves independent observational detection. By analyzing ultra-precise helioseismic data from spaceborne solar probes—such as NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO) and ESA's Solar Orbiter—astrophysicists hope to isolate distinct acoustic signatures in the Sun's deep radiative and convective boundaries, searching for the literal chemical ashes of the lost super-Earth.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1: What is a super-Earth?

A super-Earth is an exoplanet with a mass higher than Earth's but significantly lower than our solar system's ice giants (Uranus and Neptune), typically ranging between 2 and 10 times Earth's mass.

Q2: Did the Sun actually swallow a planet?

Astrophysical computer modeling from Ege University demonstrates that the Sun absorbing a 5-to-10 Earth-mass super-Earth billions of years ago accurately reproduces the Sun’s current internal sound-speed structure and depleted lithium levels, making it a strong scientific candidate.

Q3: Why doesn’t our solar system have a super-Earth today?

Super-Earths are extremely common around other stars in the galaxy. If this study is confirmed, our solar system may have originally formed one, but orbital decay forced it into the young Sun during the early formation of the planetary disk.

Q4: What is the solar lithium paradox?

The solar lithium paradox refers to the spectroscopic observation that the Sun's surface contains significantly less lithium than expected for a star of its age and mass, a discrepancy resolved if the Sun swallowed a heavy rocky planet during early accretion.

Q5: How do scientists detect what is inside the Sun?

Scientists use helioseismology, the study of acoustic sound waves reverberating through the Sun's plasma interior, measured with high precision by solar observatories like NASA's SDO and ESA's Solar Orbiter.
