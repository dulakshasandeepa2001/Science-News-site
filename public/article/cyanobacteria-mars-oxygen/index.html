Cyanobacteria That Can Produce More Oxygen Even on Mars

Scientists discover that extremophile Chroococcidiopsis cyanobacteria can survive Mars-like conditions and produce oxygen using Martian soil, with remarkable DNA repair capabilities.

✍️ By: Astrobiology Journal 📅 Published: September 11, 2025 ⏱️ Read Time: 5 min read

1.Extremophiles in Space

Extremophiles are a favorite subject of astrobiologists. These organisms not only help us understand the extreme environmental conditions that life can endure, but sometimes they also serve as very useful tools that produce oxygen needed by other organisms in those extreme environments.

2.Research Findings

In a paper published as a preprint in Acta Astronautica by Daniela Billi of the University of Rome Tor Vergata, she reviews how one extremophile acts as both a useful test subject and a useful tool for potential extraterrestrial applications, particularly in Mars colonization efforts.

3.Meet Chroococcidiopsis

The extremophile that caught Daniela's attention is a cyanobacterium called Chroococcidiopsis. This cyanobacterium can be found in deserts. Samples of these cyanobacteria have been found in regions including Asia, North America, and Antarctica (Antarctica is actually a desert too, despite being covered in ice). Since these organisms can withstand harsh environmental conditions, various research has been conducted to investigate whether they could survive on other planets.

4.Space Station Experiments

Examples of space-based research include the BIOlogy and Mars EXperiment (BIOMEX) and Biofilm Organisms Surfing Space (BOSS) experimental pair at the International Space Station, where these resilient microorganisms were exposed to conditions mimicking those on Mars.

5.Remarkable DNA Repair

After the BIOMEX experiment, when Chroococcidiopsis was brought back to Earth, it was rehydrated with water. Scientists observed that due to their DNA repair mechanisms, they were able to repair the DNA damage they had sustained. What was more interesting was that compared to strains that remained on Earth, the samples brought from space showed no increase in mutations in subsequent generations. In other words, Chroococcidiopsis's DNA repair mechanisms were extremely efficient, and they were able to return to normal conditions without any damage even after being exposed to space radiation for a year and a half without any protection.

6.Oxygen Production on Mars

It has been discovered that Chroococcidiopsis can do much more than just survive extreme conditions. It can survive in lunar and Martian soil and can produce oxygen using only that soil as a base and photosynthesis. This capability makes it a potential cornerstone for future Mars terraforming or life support systems for human missions to Mars.

7.Overcoming Perchlorate Challenge

While the high perchlorate levels in Martian soil are challenging for most Earth-based life forms, it's remarkable that these cyanobacteria can tolerate those levels. This tolerance is attributed to their exceptional DNA repair mechanisms that can resist damage caused by perchlorate levels, further demonstrating their potential utility for Mars missions.
