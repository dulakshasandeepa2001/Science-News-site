Solar Flare Sparks Northern Lights: When and Where to Watch the Upcoming Aurora Display

A powerful solar flare and coronal mass ejections are triggering geomagnetic storm watches. Here is when and where to see the Northern Lights tonight.

✍️ By: NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center 📅 Published: August 27, 2026 ⏱️ Read Time: 7 min read

1.The Solar Storm Alert: Understanding the NOAA Geomagnetic Watch

Night skies across northern latitudes are set to ignite with vibrant auroral displays as Earth intercepts an influx of high-speed solar wind and coronal mass ejections (CMEs). Following energetic solar flare activity, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's (NOAA) Space Weather Prediction Center (SWPC) has issued official geomagnetic storm watches. For skywatchers across the northern United States, Canada, and parts of Europe, this event offers an exceptional opportunity to view the dancing curtains of the Aurora Borealis. The incoming wave of charged solar particles is compressing Earth's magnetosphere, creating the conditions required for visible auroral activity. NOAA's forecast tracks conditions reaching G1 (Minor) to G2 (Moderate) storm levels. The planetary Kp index—a scale from 0 to 9 measuring geomagnetic disturbance—is projected to climb to Kp 5 or higher. A Kp index at this level pushes the auroral oval significantly southward from the Arctic circle. The strongest geomagnetic activity is expected between 8:00 p.m. and 11:00 p.m. EDT, with active auroral intervals extending into the pre-dawn hours.

2.Where Will the Northern Lights Be Visible?

While polar regions such as Alaska, northern Canada, and Scandinavia will see intense overhead curtains, mid-latitude observers along the northern tier of the United States have strong visibility potential along the northern horizon. High visibility zones include Central and Northern Canada, Alaska, and the Upper Peninsula of Michigan. Northern horizon watchers in Washington, northern Idaho, Montana, North Dakota, Minnesota, Wisconsin, and parts of Upstate New York also stand excellent chances of witnessing the display. Viewing conditions are critical: clear, dry skies away from municipal light pollution provide the highest probability of catching green and magenta hues. Urban light domes can wash out all but the most intense auroral events, so escaping to rural areas—particularly open fields, lakefronts, and designated dark-sky parks—will dramatically increase your chances of a memorable sighting. Elevation above the treeline is a bonus, as it maximizes your northern horizon exposure without obstruction.

3.The Atmospheric Science: How Solar Flares Create Auroras

The Aurora Borealis is the visual result of an intense thermodynamic and magnetic interaction occurring dozens of miles above the planet. High-energy electrons and protons ejected from solar flares travel along Earth's magnetic field lines toward the polar regions. As these solar particles collide with atmospheric atoms, energy is transferred, exciting the atoms into higher energetic states. When the atoms return to their ground state, they release photons of light in the form of aurora. The color of the aurora depends on which atmospheric gases are excited and at what altitude. Green—the most common aurora color—comes from oxygen atoms excited at altitudes around 60 to 150 miles. Crimson and deep red hues arise from high-altitude oxygen interactions above 150 miles during high-energy solar storms. Violet and blue tones are produced by nitrogen molecules reacting in the lower atmosphere. The resulting tapestry of colors is entirely determined by the energy and altitude of these particle interactions, making each aurora display a unique spectral fingerprint of the solar storm that caused it.

4.Kp Index Explained: The Key Number for Aurora Watchers

The Kp index is the standard planetary metric used by space weather agencies worldwide to communicate geomagnetic storm intensity to the public. Ranging from 0 (completely quiet magnetosphere) to 9 (extreme geomagnetic storm), each Kp level corresponds to how far south the auroral oval descends. At Kp 5, classified as a G1 Minor storm, auroras become visible from the northern United States—roughly at 50 degrees geomagnetic latitude. At Kp 6 and G2 Moderate conditions, the oval pushes further south, covering much of the northern tier of states and southern Canada. During the famous May 2024 Gannon Storm—a rare G5 event—auroras were witnessed as far south as Florida, Texas, and the Caribbean. The current event is forecast significantly below that extreme but still represents a noteworthy auroral opportunity. NOAA's SWPC updates its 3-day geomagnetic forecast multiple times per day, and real-time Kp index values are available on their website and through multiple aurora alert apps.

5.How to Spot and Photograph the Lights with a Smartphone

Modern smartphone sensors often detect subtle auroral light before it becomes fully distinct to the human eye, making camera-first observing an increasingly popular technique. To maximize your aurora photography and viewing experience, begin by finding unobstructed dark skies. Travel to open fields, lakefronts, or dark-sky parks facing true north, at least 45 minutes from city centers. Once on location, enable Night Mode on your smartphone camera: stabilize your phone on a tripod or steady mount, set the camera to Night Mode, and select a 3- to 10-second exposure. Longer exposures reveal more color depth, but require a completely steady platform to avoid blur. Allow your eyes to adjust thoroughly before deciding whether an aurora is visible: spend at least 20 minutes in total darkness away from smartphone screens, vehicle headlights, and flashlights to allow your rod photoreceptors to fully adapt. Finally, disable Wi-Fi and cellular data notifications during your session—the screen flashes during notification alerts will repeatedly reset your dark adaptation, reducing your ability to perceive faint auroral activity. Check aurora alerts before leaving home and keep the NOAA SWPC app open in low-brightness mode for real-time Kp updates throughout your session.

6.The Science of Coronal Mass Ejections and Earth's Magnetosphere

Coronal mass ejections (CMEs) are among the most energetic phenomena in the solar system. When a solar flare erupts, the magnetic field lines in the Sun's corona can snap and reconnect in a process called magnetic reconnection, releasing enormous quantities of energy and ejecting billions of tons of magnetized plasma into space at speeds ranging from 250 to over 3,000 kilometers per second. When this plasma cloud, embedded with its own magnetic field, reaches Earth after one to three days of travel, it interacts with Earth's magnetosphere in a phenomenon called a geomagnetic storm. If the CME's magnetic field is oriented southward—opposite to Earth's northward-pointing field—the two fields partially merge in a process called magnetic reconnection at Earth's magnetopause. This allows energetic particles to stream down into the polar atmosphere along magnetic field lines. The result is a compression of the night-side magnetosphere, the development of electrical current systems in the ionosphere, and the spectacular acceleration of particles into aurora-producing altitudes. The scale and duration of a storm depends on the CME's speed, density, and magnetic field orientation—all factors that NOAA's DSCOVR satellite, positioned at the L1 Lagrange point, measures in real time approximately 15 to 60 minutes before the plasma reaches Earth.

7.Solar Cycle 25 and Increasing Aurora Opportunities

The current period of elevated solar activity is not coincidental. Earth is currently experiencing Solar Cycle 25, which began in December 2019 and is tracking ahead of initial predictions in terms of solar activity intensity. Solar cycles follow an approximately 11-year pattern of rising and falling sunspot activity, with solar maximum—the peak of activity—expected to occur in 2025. During solar maximum, active regions on the Sun's surface increase dramatically in number and complexity, producing more frequent and intense solar flares and CMEs. Scientists now believe Solar Cycle 25 may be one of the most active cycles in several decades, with sunspot numbers regularly exceeding model predictions. This means that significant geomagnetic storms and associated aurora opportunities are likely to remain elevated through late 2026 and potentially into 2027 as the cycle begins its gradual descent toward solar minimum. Aurora enthusiasts may be entering a golden window for Northern Lights viewing that will not recur for another decade.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1: What triggers the Northern Lights during a solar flare?

Solar flares frequently release coronal mass ejections (CMEs)—massive clouds of magnetized plasma. When this plasma strikes Earth's magnetic field, it triggers geomagnetic storms that drive energized particles into our upper atmosphere, creating the glowing aurora.

Q2: What time is best to see the Northern Lights tonight?

The optimal viewing window typically spans from 10:00 p.m. to 2:00 a.m. local time, particularly during peak geomagnetic disturbance windows forecasted by NOAA.

Q3: Can you see the aurora borealis with the naked eye in mid-latitude states?

During G2-level storms, naked-eye viewers in mid-latitudes often perceive faint, shifting milky-grey clouds along the northern horizon, while long-exposure cameras capture the full green and violet color spectrum.
