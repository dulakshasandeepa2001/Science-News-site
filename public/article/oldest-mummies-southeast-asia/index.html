World's Oldest Mummies Discovered in Southeast Asia

Scientists have discovered 12,000-year-old mummies in Southeast Asia, making them the oldest known examples of intentional mummification in human history.

✍️ By: Archaeological Discoveries Journal 📅 Published: September 18, 2025 ⏱️ Read Time: 5 min read

1.Groundbreaking Discovery

Scientists have succeeded in discovering the world's oldest known mummies from Southeast Asia. According to them, these have been revealed to be approximately 12,000 years old, significantly predating previously known examples of mummification practices.

2.Mummification Process

Mummification preserves dead bodies by preventing decay and decomposition. Optimal environmental conditions exist in places like Chile's Atacama Desert and Ireland's wetlands where this process can occur naturally. People from various cultures and civilizations throughout history have practiced customs of mummifying the dead.

3.Historical Context

While mummies immediately bring to mind the mummified bodies of pharaohs in Egypt, even older mummified bodies have been found in various parts of the world. Until recently, the world's oldest mummified bodies were considered to be those of the Chinchorro people who lived about 7,000 years ago in what is now Peru and Chile.

4.New Research Findings

However, according to the latest study published yesterday in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences journal, not only does the record held by the oldest known mummies change, but it is said to claim a heritage dating back 12,000 years, pushing our understanding of ancient funeral practices back by several millennia.

5.Archaeological Evidence

Researchers have found human body parts deposited in limestone cave deposits from various archaeological sites in China and Vietnam, and to a lesser extent from the Philippines, Laos, Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia, some of which have been found with cut marks and burn marks, indicating intentional treatment of the dead.

6.Ancient Mummification Techniques

Upon further study of the bones, scientists discovered that these bodies were often exposed to heat. This indicated that these bodies had been smoked, dried, and mummified over fire by hunter-gatherer communities, demonstrating sophisticated ritualistic practices among some of the earliest human settlements in the region.

7.Cultural Significance

"This practice allowed people to maintain physical and spiritual connections with their ancestors, to manage time and memory," notes Hirofumi Matsumura of Sapporo Medical University in Japan, who led this study. The findings suggest that ancestor veneration and complex funeral practices were important cultural elements for these ancient communities.

8.Scientific Debate

However, regarding this study, Rita Peyroteo-Stjerna, a human evolution expert at Uppsala University in Sweden who was not involved in this research, notes that further improvements in methods for dating mummified bodies may be needed, and it is still unclear whether mummified bodies at all relevant sites in Southeast Asia were consistently smoked and dried. This highlights the ongoing scientific dialogue about these remarkable discoveries.
